# The Shape of Failure

*Why AI failures often begin before the model answers*

Every automated system receives a particular shape of the world. That shape is expressed through records, documents, events, exceptions, and missing values. If the designers have not identified those forms—and the ways they can become malformed—the machine inherits their ignorance and reproduces it at scale.

Before selecting a model, the engineer must draw the workflow as a sequence of data transformations. What enters each stage? In what form, from what source, and within what tolerances? What may be absent, corrupted, duplicated, delayed, or unlike anything previously observed? Each shape should be paired with a failure model: how it can violate expectations, how that violation will be detected, and what the workflow must do next. An unknown here is not merely an uncertainty for the machine; it is a measurement failure in the organization. The remedy is to collect the missing data or explicitly design for its absence. Otherwise, the system is being asked to operate in a world its designers have not described.

A system cannot be stabilized around a target that continues to move. The deliverable must therefore be more than an aspiration written in a prompt. It must be expressed as observable conditions and anchored to a representative corpus: examples that are acceptable, unacceptable, and genuinely ambiguous. Human reviewers should first demonstrate that they can apply those distinctions consistently. If they cannot agree on what success looks like, the model is not being measured against a specification. It is being measured against human disagreement disguised as one.

Only then does it become meaningful to place an AI model inside the workflow. The model is not the system; it is one transformation among many. Data is collected, filtered, retrieved, reformatted, presented to the model, interpreted, checked, and finally acted upon. Each transition can discard meaning or introduce error. The fluency of the final response makes the model the most conspicuous suspect, but conspicuousness is not causality. To assign blame intelligently, the entire chain must be observed, and each component must be tested at the boundary where it receives and transforms information.

Conventional tests ask whether the software succeeds under the conditions its authors anticipated. Mutation testing reverses the pressure. It deliberately introduces small faults—reversing a condition, shifting a boundary, substituting a value, or removing an operation—and then asks whether the test suite notices. A detected mutation is killed; a surviving mutation exposes a region in which the software may be wrong without the tests objecting. The testing machinery need not understand the system as an engineer does; its value is that it can apply systematic challenge beyond the limits of human patience and intuition. The resulting mutation score is not proof of correctness. It is a measurable account of how sensitive the tests are to error. In sufficiently complex software, quantified rigor is not an ornament added after human understanding; it is one of the instruments by which understanding becomes possible.

The same principle should be carried through the AI workflow. Remove an expected field. Corrupt a format. Supply stale or contradictory source material. Perturb the phrasing of an instruction. Interrupt retrieval. Substitute a fluent but incorrect model response. At every boundary, ask whether the surrounding system detects the disturbance, abstains, falls back, or routes the case to a human. These experiments do not merely test whether the model can answer; they test whether the organization can recognize when its machinery should not be trusted. Where intuition becomes exhausted, repeatable measurement must take over. A robust AI workflow is not one that never fails, but one that makes failure visible before it becomes action.

Only after this discipline has been applied does the phrase *AI failure* acquire useful meaning. If the input was well formed, the deliverable was stable, the supporting components behaved correctly, and the model still violated a known requirement, then the model has failed within the conditions established for it. But if the data was absent, the target disputed, the retrieval defective, or the safeguards unable to recognize error, the model may merely be the place where an earlier failure became visible. Calling that an AI failure does not improve the system. It interrupts the feedback by which the organization might learn.

A generative model is an engine of learned resemblance. It can produce behavior that looks remarkably like understanding, but resemblance cannot carry responsibility. People choose the objective, define acceptable evidence, engineer the transitions, and decide what must happen when uncertainty enters the system. The useful question is therefore not whether the fault belongs exclusively to the human or the AI. It is whether the human-built system gave every component what it needed and made every limitation observable. Before declaring that the AI failed, demonstrate that the system knew what success meant, knew the shape of its world, and knew how to recognize when it was wrong. If it did not, the failure began long before the model answered.

## The Computational Expression

This argument is accompanied by [an executable reference implementation](computational-expression/README.md). It uses Hypothesis to generate data and sequences, Mutmut to challenge the safety contract, OpenRouter as an optional live model boundary, and a visible weave of specialized agents that fails closed when an assumption breaks. The code cannot prove that every AI failure is human-made. It can prove whether this particular system knows what it accepts, what it rejects, and where a failure first becomes observable.
